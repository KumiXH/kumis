by Learning to Re-render Synthetic Faces

Abstract
We introduce SynthLight, a diffusion model for portrait
relighting. We frame image relighting as a re-rendering
problem, where pixels are transformed in response to
changes in environmental lighting. Using a physically-
based rendering engine, we create a dataset to simulate
this lighting-conditioned transformation with 3D head as-
sets under varying lighting. We propose training and in-
ference strategies to bridge the gap between the synthetic
and real image domains: (1) multi-task training leveraging
real human portraits without lighting labels; (2) an infer-
ence time diffusion sampling scheme based on classifier-
free guidance leveraging the input portrait to better pre-
serve details. Our method generalizes to diverse real por-
traits and produces realistic illumination effects, includ-
ing specular highlights and cast shadows, while preserving
identity. Our quantitative experiments on light stage data
demonstrate results comparable to state-of-the-art relight-
ing methods. Our qualitative results on in-the-wild images
showcase rich and unprecedented illumination effects.

1. Introduction
Lighting is fundamental to portrait photography, yet ma-
nipulating it after capture remains challenging. Recent ad-
vances in generative imaging models have shown promise
for controlling lighting in existing images [14, 18, 34,
58, 60], but these typically require labeled training data.
For portrait relighting specifically, the most effective re-
sults have come from training on light stage data—portraits
rendered with linear combinations of one-light-at-a-time
(OLAT) captures. While powerful, light stage setups are
constrained by physical limitations in light source den-
sity and require specialized artificial lighting equipment.
In contrast, 3D workflows in VFX and gaming have long
treated lighting as a straightforward endeavor through mod-
ern physically based rendering engines, where lighting con-
trol is nearly arbitrary. To relight a rendering, artists simply
adjust the lighting configurations and re-render the scene.
Given a sceneS and lighting L1, we denote the rendering
as I1 = fr(S, L1). The inverse graphics problem aims to
find S from I1: S = finv(I1, L1) with known or unknown
lighting. To relight renderingI1 to I2 under lighting L2, one
aims to compute: I2 = fr(S, L2). Given only I1, a relight-

ing procedure seeks: I2 = fr(finv(I1), L2) = fre(I1, L2),
where fre is the relighting/re-rendering function. Previ-
ous approaches [21, 28, 57] have tackled this problem
through inverse graphics, explicitly or implicitly, by es-
timating lighting-invariant intrinsic scene representations
such as depth, surface normals, and albedo. This imposes
limitations on subsequent rendering functions and fails to
capture complex illumination effects like inter-reflections,
occlusion shadows and subsurface scattering. In this paper,
we propose bypassing inverse rendering by learning the re-
lighting function using physically based 3D renderings of
human heads. We render pairs of portrait images in Blender
(Cycles) (I1, L1) and (I2, L2) and train a diffusion model
to directly learn to “re-render” I2 from I1 and L2.
However, this approach introduces an inevitable domain
gap between 3D renders and real photographs. To address
this challenge, we leverage a latent diffusion model pre-
trained on vast internet images for text-to-image generation.
We propose to finetune the network with our face render-
ings and introduce simple yet effective schemes to narrow
the gap between training data and in-the-wild images. Dur-
ing training, we propose multi-task training that incorpo-
rates in-the-wild images without ground truth relighting in-
formation. This allows the model to learn relighting from
our synthetic dataset while maintaining knowledge of the
real image domain, preventing distributional drift. We fur-
ther observe that input portraits contain rich textural infor-
mation. Leveraging the flexibility of diffusion model in-
ference, we design an inference adaptation scheme that
effectively preserves input portrait details in the relit result.
We evaluate our methods on in-the-wild portrait images,
demonstrating highly detailed illumination effects that ac-
curately capture interactions between the portrait scene and
lighting. Our results produce realistic cast shadows and
specular highlights on the skin. For the first time, we
demonstrate an end-to-end system capable of non-trivial
lighting effects including catch lights in eyes, subsurface
scattering in ears, and inter-reflections with clothing. No-
tably, despite training only on simple headshot renderings
of 3D faces without accessories, facial hair, or hats, our
network generalizes to complex portrait images, including
half-body shots and multi-person photographs.
We quantitatively evaluate our method on a test set of our
synthetic faces dataset and on a light stage OLAT dataset.
Despite using no light stage data for training, our method
achieves comparable or superior results to state-of-the-art
portrait relighting methods trained on OLAT data. User
studies show our results are preferred across all evaluated
aspects: perceptual lighting accuracy, identity preservation,
and overall image quality.
We summarize our contributions as follows:
1. We propose modeling portrait relighting as a task of
learning to re-render a portrait scene in 3D. Using phys-
ically based renderings of human heads under varying
lighting conditions, we train a diffusion model to learn
pixel transformations conditioned on lighting.
2. We introduce two techniques enabling synthetic data
learning while minimizing domain gap with real images,
through a training-time multi-task strategy that incorpo-
rates real images through a text-to-image task, and an
inference-time approach based on classifier-free guid-
ance that preserves portrait details in the relit result.
3. Through extensive qualitative and quantitative evalua-
tions, we demonstrate state-of-the-art portrait relight-
ing results, achieving high-quality lighting effects pre-
viously unattainable by existing methods.
2. Related Work
2.1. Portrait Relighting
Portrait relighting has been explored in both 2D [18,21, 28,
29, 34, 44, 47, 53, 58, 60] and 3D [3, 6, 33, 49, 50, 52, 62],
with 2D image-based methods being more relevant to our
work. Due to the under-constrained nature of 2D portrait
relighting, various priors have been proposed, including
morphable 3D face models [4, 43], explicit inverse render-
ing [2, 41], and style transfer [42].
Recently, deep learning methods [27,47] trained on light
stage data [9] have advanced the state-of-the-art. Total Re-
lighting [28] is a widely adopted physics-guided method
that decomposes images into intrinsic components condi-
tioned on HDR environment lighting [8]. However, this
approach has two limitations. First, its BRDF-based re-
flectance model [7,31] neglects other significant light trans-
port modes such as subsurface scattering prevalent in cer-
tain skin types [11, 22, 26], and struggles with albedo esti-
mation in cases involving face accessories, inter-reflections,
and face paint [21, 57]. Second, light stage setups inher-
ently limit captured lighting complexity due to restricted
intensity and resolution [47, 48], affecting the modeling of
complex effects such as subsurface scattering.
Motivated by these constraints, we employ diffusion
models for face relighting, without predefined appearance
assumptions. We use synthetic datasets rendered with
physically-based methods to provide paired supervision.
Our approach can synthesize advanced illumination effects
in human portraits, such as hard cast shadows, subsurface
scattering, and inter-reflections.
2.2. Diffusion Models for Relighting
Diffusion models [1, 10, 19, 20, 35, 37, 38, 45, 46] have
become standard for text-to-image generation, image trans-
lation, and appearance editing. Their scalability on large
datasets and adaptability of pretrained weights [17, 35, 59]
make them particularly effective. Additionally, flexible
sampling mechanisms can enhance image quality [15, 24].

Recent diffusion-based relighting methods include Di-
LightNet [58], which provides fine-grained control of object
lighting but relies on error-prone scene reconstruction [51],
and Neural Gaffer [18], which utilizes HDR environment
maps for object relighting. For portraits, Relightful Har-
monization [34] and IC-Light [60] train on high-quality
data (light stage captures, Objaverse renders, composited
shadow materials) for background-harmonized outputs, us-
ing background as lighting condition. Diffusion Face Re-
lighting [32] employs conditional diffusion implicit models
with spherical harmonic lighting but is limited to tight fa-
cial crops. In contrast, we approach portrait relighting by
learning to re-render synthetic faces. Leveraging pretrained
models and our multi-task training strategy, we preserve fa-
cial priors, while classifier-free guidance [15] on the input
portrait enhances textures and details in the relit results.
2.3. Domain Adaptation
Training naively on synthetic data creates a domain gap
for in-the-wild portraits, harming identity preservation and
photo-realism. Prior diffusion-based domain adaptation
methods [12, 17, 36, 56, 59] primarily target style transfer
or specific editing, not relighting.
DifFRelight [14] trains personalized diffusion models
per subject, preserving identity but requires light stage cap-
ture and dedicated per-subject training. Other approaches
use real data to reduce the synthetic-to-real gap: Switch-
Light [21] pre-trains a masked-autoencoder [13] on real
images before light stage training to learn visual features
essential for relighting (e.g., structure, color, texture); Re-
lightful Harmonization [34] bootstraps from a light stage
trained model to pseudo-label in-the-wild images, then fine-
tunes on these labels; IC-Light [60] employs extensive
data augmentation; and Lumos [57] finetunes its albedo-
prediction branch on real images but struggles with face
paint, accessories, or strong shadows.
We propose a multi-task scheme combining text-to-
image and relighting, enabling diffusion model training
with real and synthetic images. Our classifier-free guidance
inference preserves input portrait details. Our user study
demonstrates superior visual quality, identity preservation,
and lighting compared to existing methods.
3. Method
Given a portrait image I captured under unknown lighting,
our goal is to synthesize a relit portraitIR under target light-
ing specified by a panoramic environment map E. The relit
portrait IR should (1) preserve the subject’s identity and fa-
cial characteristics from I; (2) reflect the lighting defined
by E; and (3) maintain photorealism. We first simulate this
re-rendering in Blender to build a synthetic portrait dataset.

3.1. Synthetic Data for Relighting
We build a 3D human portrait generation pipeline similar to
FaceSynthetics [54]. Our system begins with artist-created
3D head meshes, enhanced with detailed facial components,
including eyes, teeth, gums, and hair. We augment these
base models through rigging for pose variation and blend-
shape deformation for diverse facial expressions. For re-
alistic rendering, we use high-quality PBR texture maps,
including albedo, normal, roughness, specular, and subsur-
face scattering. We combine the head with random clothing
meshes to build portrait scenes using Blender and render
images with Cycles.
To train our networks, we render images (samples in
Fig. 2) at 512×512 resolution from 350 subjects, each
with roughly 10 varied appearances, including different
hairstyles, skin tones, expressions, clothes, and poses. Each
sample is rendered with 10 random HDR environment
maps, rotated evenly 36 times from a random initial rota-
tion. In total, the dataset contains approximately 1.26 mil-
lion images. See supplemental material for more samples.
3.2. Modeling Relighting with Diffusion Model
We build on top of Stable Diffusion [35], a text-to-image
foundation model pretrained with vast internet data. As
shown in Fig.3, we incorporate the input portrait I and the
target environment map E to the input of the network back-
bone by expanding the number of channels in the first con-
volutional layer of the Unet as per Palette[37], and we train
the diffusion model to generate IR, the relit portrait.
To generate training samples (I, E, T, I R), where T is a
text prompt, we render portrait images from a subject with
different HDR maps to obtain portraits n portraits I1 · · · In.

tion these images. Training samples are constructed by

get portrait as (Ii, Ej, Tj, Ij). We use the sample to su-
pervise our diffusion model in the following manner. First,
we convert the HDR environment map into LDR by tone-
mapping similar to Neural Gaffer [18]. The LDR envi-

3.3. Multitask Training
Fine-tuning a diffusion model on synthetic data creates a
domain gap when applied to in-the-wild images, degrading
output quality. For instance, the model often fails to repro-
duce critical details, such as textures in clothing, jewelry
and accessories, which are absent in the synthetic data dis-
tribution (e.g., as seen in the Base result in Fig. 9).
To address this, we propose multitask training to miti-
gate distribution drift toward synthetic renderings. Similar
strategies have been applied in the context of inpainting [55]
to compensate for limited training data diversity.
Specifically, we incorporate a text-to-portrait generation
task that constrains the diffusion model to produce realis-
tic portrait images from input prompts. This task is trained
alongside the original relighting task. As both tasks share
the same network architecture, we simply replace the image
and LDR inputs with two black images, as shown in Fig. 3.
For text-to-portrait training, we curate a subset of hu-
man portraits from the LAION [40] dataset, filtered with a
face detector. Details on detection and filtering are provided
in the supplementary material. During training, we set the
sampling ratio of synthetic to real data to 0.7 and 0.3, re-
spectively. Incorporating real images significantly improves

4. Experiments
4.1. Setup and Metrics
We create three test sets for evaluating our method: (a) 300
light stage rendered relighting pairs, (b) a held out subset of
our synthetic faces dataset consisting of 500 images, (c) in-
the-wild portraits for qualitative evaluation of visual quality.
We use quantitative metrics such as SSIM, PSNR, LPIPS
[61] to evaluate image fidelity and face embedding distance
from FaceNet [39] to evaluate identity preservation. We
train on the entire synthetic dataset but withhold 20% of the
environment maps to create the light stage test set. We also
hold out 10% of the subjects and 10% of the environment
maps for the synthetic test set, ensuring they remain unseen
during training.
4.2. Implementation details
We implement our model in PyTorch [30] and train on 32×
40GB A100 GPUs. We use a batch size of 192, a learning
rate of 10−5, and the Adam optimizer [23]. Training (in-
cluding ablations) runs for 40K steps, taking about 1 day.
Model Initialization We initialize our model using either
the IC-Light checkpoint [60] or the Stable Diffusion 1.5
checkpoint [35]. Although both initializations yield visu-
ally similar results on in-the-wild data and our light stage
test set (Fig. 10), the model initialized from the Stable Dif-
fusion 1.5 checkpoint achieves slightly lower scores on both
the synthetic and light stage test sets (Tab.3). Consequently,
results in this paper are from our model initialized with the
IC-Light checkpoint, unless otherwise specified.
4.3. Evaluation Results
We compare our method to state-of-the-art approaches in
portrait harmonization [60], portrait relighting [21], and ob-
ject relighting [18, 58], evaluating both quantitatively (see
Tab.1) on the synthetic and light stage test sets, and qualita-
tively (see Fig. 7 and Fig. 8). Quantitative evaluation shows
that our method outperforms baselines on the synthetic test
set and performs comparably to state-of-the-art portrait re-
lighting methods like SwitchLight on the light stage test set.
While our results do not always achieve the highest PSNR,
they exhibit better visual relighting quality than baselines.
In Fig. 7, we show the input image (left), the environment
map (top), and the corresponding 3D model rendered in
Blender (bottom). In Fig. 8, the left side shows the envi-
ronment map (top) and input portrait (bottom).
We conduct a user study (see Tab.2) to evaluate percep-
tual preference in relighting. We select 40 held-out stock
portraits, each paired with a randomly sampled environment
map from a test set of 200. Each portrait is relit under 12
evenly spaced rotations of the environment map, generat-
ing 480 relit images per method. For the study, we ran-
domly sample 30 image pairs per participant, each compar-
ing our method to a baseline. Participants answer three 2-
alternative forced-choice questions assessing (1) lighting,
(2) image quality, and (3) identity preservation. We col-
lect 482 responses from 20 participants. Results show our
method is consistently preferred across all criteria. See the
supplementary for screenshots illustrating our user study.

4.5. Effect of Lighting Condition
We train two variants of our model: one conditioned on
background and the other on the environment map for light-
ing. While the background-conditioned model often pro-
duces plausible lighting (e.g., strong cast shadows) and
appears well harmonized with the background, rotating
the environment map reveals lighting inconsistencies (see
Fig. 11). Although generating realistic shadows caused by
self-occlusion is challenging for background-conditioned
relighting methods such as IC-Light [60], leveraging our
synthetic dataset enables us to address this effectively.

5. Conclusion
We present SynthLight, a portrait relighting diffusion model
capable of relighting in-the-wild portraits while garnering
lighting supervision only from synthetic data. Our method
demonstrates strong generalization capabilities, underscor-
ing the potential of synthetic data to achieve plausible light-
ing effects such as strong cast shadows, realistic catch lights
in the eyes, and natural inter-reflections. We discuss limita-
tions and future work in the supplementary.
